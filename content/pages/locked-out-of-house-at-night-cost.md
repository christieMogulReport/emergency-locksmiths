**Short version:** if everyone is safe, check the other doors, call anyone who has a key, and if you still need a locksmith, expect to pay roughly **$150 to $250 for an after-hours home lockout** in much of the U.S. A quote of $15 to $45 is the classic bait-and-switch. Get the **full price in writing before anyone starts work.**

## If someone is in danger, stop here

Call **911** if a child, a vulnerable adult, or a pet is locked inside, someone inside needs urgent medication, or there is a fire or gas hazard (a stove left on, for example). Police and fire crews treat that as an emergency. For an ordinary locked door with nobody at risk, a locksmith is the right call, not the police ([Vivint](https://www.vivint.com/resources/article/locked-out-of-house-what-to-do), [Public Locksmith](https://blog.publiclocks.com/locked-out-of-house-what-to-do)).

## Steps to take right now (in order)

1. **Walk the outside and check every other way in.** Back and side doors, a sliding door, an unlocked garage side door, or a ground-floor window you may have left unlatched. Do not climb to a second story and do not break glass: the repair usually costs more than a locksmith visit ([Public Locksmith](https://blog.publiclocks.com/locked-out-of-house-what-to-do)).
2. **Call anyone who might have a key.** Roommates, family, a neighbor, a pet sitter or cleaner. If you rent, call your landlord or property manager. They often have a key, but some charge an after-hours fee or have rules about calling your own locksmith, so check your lease ([Public Locksmith](https://blog.publiclocks.com/locked-out-of-house-what-to-do), [AAA](https://mwg.aaa.com/home-security/articles/what-to-do-when-locked-out-of-your-house)).
3. **Check a smart lock or keypad.** Try the app or the backup code before paying for a service call.
4. **Skip the force.** Prying the door or frame can split the jamb and ruin the lock, turning a lockout into a repair bill. The credit-card trick only works on a spring latch, never on a deadbolt.
5. **If you do get in another way, note how easy it was.** If you could get in, so could someone else.
6. **Still locked out? Call two or three locksmiths** (see the phone questions below) and have photo ID or proof of address ready. A legitimate locksmith will want proof that you live there ([Public Locksmith](https://blog.publiclocks.com/locked-out-of-house-what-to-do)). Wait somewhere safe and lit, not at your front door, if it is late.

Prices under about $100 at night deserve questions, and prices of $15 to $45 are a warning sign, not a bargain (see below). These are our reading of the sources above, not a guarantee for your city.

## Locksmith scam warning signs

Locksmith scams are a well-documented "bait-and-switch." Government and consumer groups describe the same pattern again and again:

- **A rock-bottom phone quote.** The Better Business Bureau describes quotes "as low as $15." The South Carolina Department of Consumer Affairs says a quote of $10 or $20 "is indicative of a scam." In a lawsuit, the Ohio Attorney General said consumers were told $19 and the real price "regularly turned out to be much higher" ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/article/news-releases/22797-bbb-scam-alert-locked-out-dont-fall-for-a-locksmith-cons), [SC Consumer Affairs](https://www.consumer.sc.gov/sites/consumer/files/Documents/News/2014/14008.pdf), [Ohio AG](https://www.ohioattorneygeneral.gov/Media/Newsletters/Consumer-Advocate/August-2016/Beware-Searching-for-Local-Locksmiths-May-Lead)). The low number is only the trip fee, and the "labor" and "complications" get added on arrival.
- **"We have to drill the lock."** The BBB says it isn't routine to insist on drilling and that most locksmiths can open almost any lock. Drilling forces you to buy a new lock, often an overpriced one ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/article/news-releases/22797-bbb-scam-alert-locked-out-dont-fall-for-a-locksmith-cons)). Drilling can be legitimate for a broken or high-security lock, so ask why and how much the lock will cost *before* they start.
- **A generic phone greeting.** "Locksmith services" or "Locksmith" instead of a specific business name can mean a call center ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/article/news-releases/22797-bbb-scam-alert-locked-out-dont-fall-for-a-locksmith-cons)).
- **A "local" business that isn't.** The FTC has warned that many "local" listings route to an out-of-state call center, with addresses where no locksmith can be found ([FTC alert, reproduced](https://rockymountainsecuritygroup.com/files/HIRE_A_LOCKSMITH.pdf), [KUTV on the FTC alert](https://kutv.com/news/get-gephardt/ftc-red-flags-point-to-signs-a-locksmith-should-be-avoided)). Connecticut's consumer protection department says to look up the street address on a map and hang up if the person cannot give you one ([CT DCP, July 16, 2026](https://portal.ct.gov/dcp/news-releases-from-the-department-of-consumer-protection/2026-news-releases/be-wary-of-scammers-posing-as-locksmith-technicians)).
- **An unmarked vehicle and no ID.** Connecticut DCP: scammers often arrive in unmarked vehicles, with no uniform and no identification. You can refuse service.
- **A price that changes at your door.** The FTC advice: if the on-site price does not match the phone estimate, do not allow the work. Never sign a blank work order ([KUTV on the FTC alert](https://kutv.com/news/get-gephardt/ftc-red-flags-point-to-signs-a-locksmith-should-be-avoided), [Minnesota AG](https://www.ag.state.mn.us/consumer/publications/hiringalocksmith.asp)).
- **Cash only, and pressure to pay now.** Connecticut DCP lists high-pressure tactics and demands for cash as common. The BBB suggests paying by credit card so you can dispute a charge.
- **Fake reviews.** Connecticut DCP warns that scammers pay for fake Google reviews. It suggests checking the BBB and Yelp, looking for lots of reviews in a short time, one-review accounts, and vague five-star reviews, and searching the business name plus "scam" or "complaint."

## What to ask on the phone

Call two or three places if you can. Write down the answers.

1. **"What is the exact name of your business and your street address?"** Look it up while you talk. Hang up if they cannot say.
2. **"What is the total price to open a standard residential door, including the trip fee, labor, mileage, and after-hours charge?"** Ask for a number or a firm maximum, not just "starts at."
3. **"Is that price the most I will pay if the lock is not damaged?"** And: **"What would make it cost more, and what would that cost?"**
4. **"Do you drill the lock first?"** A skilled locksmith should try to open it without damage. Ask what they charge for a replacement lock if drilling is unavoidable.
5. **"How long until you arrive, and what name will the technician give? What does your vehicle look like?"**
6. **"Are you licensed here?"** Some states license locksmiths and others do not, so ask about your state's rule. In Connecticut, for example, each individual locksmith must be registered with the state.
7. **"Will you give me a written, itemized invoice, and do you take credit cards?"**

When they arrive: check the name on the vehicle, their ID, and the business name on the invoice against the company you called. Get the price confirmed again **before** they touch the lock. If it doesn't match the phone quote, you can send them away and call someone else, though you may owe a fair trip fee, so ask on the phone what that is.

## After you are back inside

- **Pay by credit card if you can,** and keep the itemized invoice (parts, labor, mileage, service charge, business name and address) so you can dispute a charge ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/article/news-releases/22797-bbb-scam-alert-locked-out-dont-fall-for-a-locksmith-cons)).
- **If you think you were scammed,** file a complaint with your state attorney general or consumer protection office, the BBB, and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
- **Save a trusted locksmith's number in your phone now.** The FTC's advice is to find a locksmith before you need one ([KUTV on the FTC alert](https://kutv.com/news/get-gephardt/ftc-red-flags-point-to-signs-a-locksmith-should-be-avoided)).
- **Lost your keys rather than just left them inside?** Anyone who finds them could know your address. Ask about rekeying, which usually costs less than replacing the lock ($50 to $130 per lock, per Angi).
