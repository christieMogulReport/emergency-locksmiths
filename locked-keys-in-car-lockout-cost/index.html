Locked keys in your car: what to do and what an emergency car lockout costs

Last updated November 18, 2026

Short version: if a child or pet is inside, call 911 now. Otherwise, before you pay anyone, check whether you already have roadside assistance (AAA, your car insurance, or your car’s warranty), because many plans cover a lockout. If you do need a locksmith, published cost guides put a plain car lockout at roughly $60 to $150 in business hours and about $150 to $250 or more at night (HomeGuide, Forbes Home). Get the total price, including the trip fee, before anyone is sent. Call to confirm.

If a child or pet is inside, call 911 first

  • A child locked in a car is an emergency. NHTSA says that if a child is alone in a locked car, act immediately and call 911. A child’s body temperature rises three to five times faster than an adult’s, and a child in distress from heat should be removed and cooled as quickly as possible (NHTSA). Do not wait for a locksmith or for roadside assistance to arrive.
  • A pet locked in a car can overheat fast, too. The Humane Veterinary Medical Alliance says that when it is 72 degrees outside, the inside of a car can reach 116 degrees within an hour, even with the windows open and in the shade. For an animal in a hot car, it advises calling your local police or animal control and waiting by the car (Humane World for Animals / HumaneVMA fact sheet). If the animal looks to be in distress, call 911.
  • The engine is running, or the car is somewhere unsafe (a highway shoulder, a dark lot late at night): tell the dispatcher or roadside service that, and wait somewhere safe and lit.

Step by step: what to do right now

  1. Make sure everyone is safe. See above. If a child or animal is inside, stop and call 911.
  2. Check every door and the trunk. One door or the hatch may not have locked. Some trunks open from a separate button or key slot.
  3. Look for a spare key. Someone at home, a partner, a roommate, or a coworker may be able to bring one. If home is close, a ride there and back can cost less than a night call-out (several people on Reddit say they wish they had done exactly that after paying a large bill).
  4. Try your car’s app if it has one. Many newer cars can be unlocked remotely through the carmaker’s app or connected-car service, if you set it up before. Check your owner’s manual or the app.
  5. Check roadside assistance before you call a locksmith. The FTC’s advice: if you are locked out of your car and have a roadside assistance service, call them first. These services sometimes come with a new car, as an add-on through your insurer, or as a separate membership (FTC alert, reproduced). See the coverage section below for what AAA and some insurers say they include.
  6. Skip the coat hanger and the slim jim. Forbes Home warns that prying a modern car open with a hanger is likely to cause damage and might not even work (Forbes Home). Wires and wedges can scratch paint, tear weather seals, or damage the wiring inside the door. Breaking a window turns a lockout into a glass bill.
  7. If you still need a locksmith, call two or three and ask the questions below. Have your driver’s licence and proof you own the car ready (registration or an insurance card, on your phone if the paper copy is in the glovebox). A legitimate locksmith will ask for proof that the car is yours (Forbes Home).
  8. Get the full price before they come, and again before they touch the car. If the price at the car doesn’t match the phone quote, you can say no.

Check whether you’re already covered

A lot of drivers pay out of pocket for a lockout their membership or policy would have covered. Coverage, limits, and rules vary by plan and by state, so call your provider to confirm what your plan includes.

Insurer roadside coverage only applies if it is on your policy; check your declarations page or app. Thumbtack also suggests checking your insurance, warranty, or roadside program before you pay a locksmith (Thumbtack).

What a car lockout costs if you pay a locksmith

There is no single national price. These sources measure different things:

What to take from this: for an ordinary car with the key visible on the seat, something around $75 to $150 in the daytime and about $150 to $250 at night is a reasonable expectation in much of the U.S., based on the sources above. Big cities, long drives, holidays, and some luxury or high-security cars can cost more. A phone quote of $15 to $35 is a warning sign, not a bargain (see below). This is our reading of published averages, not a price for your town, so call to confirm.

Locked in vs. lost. If the key is inside the car, it’s a lockout. If the key is lost or broken, you need a new key cut and programmed, which is a different and pricier job. The Locksmith Ledger survey puts transponder key programming at about $88 and an “all keys lost” job at about $228 on average (Locksmith Ledger). Lost-key replacement will get its own page.

What people complain about (and how to avoid it)

We read recent posts on X and lockout threads on Reddit. The same few themes keep coming up:

  • “It took 30 seconds and cost $250 to $375.” Several people describe paying well above the ranges above, often after calling the first company in the search results late at night or on a weekend. Experienced locksmiths in those threads often answer that a fast job is normal, but the price should have been agreed up front.
  • “They quoted a $35 ‘service fee’ on the phone, then the bill was much higher.” This is the classic bait-and-switch (see the next section).
  • “They wouldn’t give me a price on the phone.” Locksmiths replying in those threads call a refusal to quote a car unlock by phone a red flag.
  • “An unmarked van, the card on the invoice didn’t match, and the number was disconnected.” People worry afterward about having handed their card details to an unknown business.
  • “I paid and then found out my roadside plan would have covered it.” Check your coverage first (above).
  • “He couldn’t open it and still charged me.” Ask on the phone what you owe if they can’t get in.

Locksmith scam warning signs

Government and consumer groups describe the same pattern:

  • A rock-bottom phone quote. The BBB describes quotes “as low as $15” that grow once the locksmith arrives, as they find “reasons” to raise the price (BBB). The low number is usually only the trip fee.
  • A “local” company that isn’t. The FTC has warned that some “local” listings route to a call center in another city, sometimes under many different names, with a listed address where no locksmith shop exists. Once someone arrives, often in an unmarked vehicle, they may want much more money and accept only cash (FTC alert, reproduced).
  • A generic phone greeting. “Locksmith services” instead of a business name can mean a call center (BBB).
  • No uniform, no ID, unmarked vehicle, cash and pressure. Connecticut’s consumer protection department lists these as common signs and suggests looking up the street address on a map (CT DCP, July 16, 2026).
  • A price that changes at the car. If the on-site price doesn’t match the phone estimate, don’t allow the work, and never sign a blank work order (Minnesota AG).
  • Fake reviews. Connecticut DCP warns that scammers pay for fake reviews. Look for lots of reviews in a short time, one-review accounts, and vague five-star reviews, and search the business name plus “scam” or “complaint” (CT DCP).

What to ask on the phone

  1. “What is your business name and street address?” Look it up while you talk. A mobile-only locksmith can be legitimate, but should say so and give a business name (FTC alert, reproduced).
  2. “What is the total to unlock a [year, make, model] with the key inside, including the trip fee, any night or weekend charge, and mileage?” Ask for a number or a firm maximum, not “starts at.”
  3. “What do I owe if you can’t open it?”
  4. “How long until you arrive? What name will the technician give, and what will the vehicle look like?”
  5. “Do you take credit cards, and will I get an itemized invoice with your business name and address?”

When they arrive, check that the name on the vehicle, the technician’s ID, and the invoice match the company you called, and confirm the price before they start.

Afterward

  • Pay by credit card if you can, and keep the itemized invoice. The BBB suggests a card so you can dispute a charge (BBB). If you have roadside coverage with reimbursement, you’ll need the receipt to claim it.
  • If you think you were scammed, report it to your state attorney general or consumer protection office, the BBB, and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If you’re worried about the card you used, call your card issuer.
  • Prevent the next one. Keep a spare key somewhere you can get to (not hidden on the car), save your roadside provider’s number and your membership or policy number in your phone, and save a locksmith you have checked. The FTC suggests researching a locksmith before you need one (FTC alert, reproduced).
