Emergency Locksmith Guide

Locked out at 2 a.m.? Real prices, scam warnings, and what to ask before anyone comes to your door.

Locked out of your house at night: what to do and what an emergency locksmith costs

Last updated November 18, 2026

Short version: if everyone is safe, check the other doors, call anyone who has a key, and if you still need a locksmith, expect to pay roughly $150 to $250 for an after-hours home lockout in much of the U.S. A quote of $15 to $45 is the classic bait-and-switch. Get the full price in writing before anyone starts work.

If someone is in danger, stop here

Call 911 if a child, a vulnerable adult, or a pet is locked inside, someone inside needs urgent medication, or there is a fire or gas hazard (a stove left on, for example). Police and fire crews treat that as an emergency. For an ordinary locked door with nobody at risk, a locksmith is the right call, not the police (Vivint, Public Locksmith).

Steps to take right now (in order)

  1. Walk the outside and check every other way in. Back and side doors, a sliding door, an unlocked garage side door, or a ground-floor window you may have left unlatched. Do not climb to a second story and do not break glass: the repair usually costs more than a locksmith visit (Public Locksmith).
  2. Call anyone who might have a key. Roommates, family, a neighbor, a pet sitter or cleaner. If you rent, call your landlord or property manager. They often have a key, but some charge an after-hours fee or have rules about calling your own locksmith, so check your lease (Public Locksmith, AAA).
  3. Check a smart lock or keypad. Try the app or the backup code before paying for a service call.
  4. Skip the force. Prying the door or frame can split the jamb and ruin the lock, turning a lockout into a repair bill. The credit-card trick only works on a spring latch, never on a deadbolt.
  5. If you do get in another way, note how easy it was. If you could get in, so could someone else.
  6. Still locked out? Call two or three locksmiths (see the phone questions below) and have photo ID or proof of address ready. A legitimate locksmith will want proof that you live there (Public Locksmith). Wait somewhere safe and lit, not at your front door, if it is late.

What to take from this: at 2 a.m., a fair all-in price for a plain home lockout in much of the country is likely somewhere around $150 to $250, and it can run higher in big cities, in rural areas where the locksmith drives far, or on holidays. Prices under about $100 at night deserve questions, and prices of $15 to $45 are a warning sign, not a bargain (see below). These are our reading of the sources above, not a guarantee for your city.

Locksmith scam warning signs

Locksmith scams are a well-documented “bait-and-switch.” Government and consumer groups describe the same pattern again and again:

  • A rock-bottom phone quote. The Better Business Bureau describes quotes “as low as $15.” The South Carolina Department of Consumer Affairs says a quote of $10 or $20 “is indicative of a scam.” In a lawsuit, the Ohio Attorney General said consumers were told $19 and the real price “regularly turned out to be much higher” (BBB, SC Consumer Affairs, Ohio AG). The low number is only the trip fee, and the “labor” and “complications” get added on arrival.
  • “We have to drill the lock.” The BBB says it isn’t routine to insist on drilling and that most locksmiths can open almost any lock. Drilling forces you to buy a new lock, often an overpriced one (BBB). Drilling can be legitimate for a broken or high-security lock, so ask why and how much the lock will cost before they start.
  • A generic phone greeting. “Locksmith services” or “Locksmith” instead of a specific business name can mean a call center (BBB).
  • A “local” business that isn’t. The FTC has warned that many “local” listings route to an out-of-state call center, with addresses where no locksmith can be found (FTC alert, reproduced, KUTV on the FTC alert). Connecticut’s consumer protection department says to look up the street address on a map and hang up if the person cannot give you one (CT DCP, July 16, 2026).
  • An unmarked vehicle and no ID. Connecticut DCP: scammers often arrive in unmarked vehicles, with no uniform and no identification. You can refuse service.
  • A price that changes at your door. The FTC advice: if the on-site price does not match the phone estimate, do not allow the work. Never sign a blank work order (KUTV on the FTC alert, Minnesota AG).
  • Cash only, and pressure to pay now. Connecticut DCP lists high-pressure tactics and demands for cash as common. The BBB suggests paying by credit card so you can dispute a charge.
  • Fake reviews. Connecticut DCP warns that scammers pay for fake Google reviews. It suggests checking the BBB and Yelp, looking for lots of reviews in a short time, one-review accounts, and vague five-star reviews, and searching the business name plus “scam” or “complaint.”

What to ask on the phone

Call two or three places if you can. Write down the answers.

  1. “What is the exact name of your business and your street address?” Look it up while you talk. Hang up if they cannot say.
  2. “What is the total price to open a standard residential door, including the trip fee, labor, mileage, and after-hours charge?” Ask for a number or a firm maximum, not just “starts at.”
  3. “Is that price the most I will pay if the lock is not damaged?” And: “What would make it cost more, and what would that cost?”
  4. “Do you drill the lock first?” A skilled locksmith should try to open it without damage. Ask what they charge for a replacement lock if drilling is unavoidable.
  5. “How long until you arrive, and what name will the technician give? What does your vehicle look like?”
  6. “Are you licensed here?” Some states license locksmiths and others do not, so ask about your state’s rule. In Connecticut, for example, each individual locksmith must be registered with the state.
  7. “Will you give me a written, itemized invoice, and do you take credit cards?”

When they arrive: check the name on the vehicle, their ID, and the business name on the invoice against the company you called. Get the price confirmed again before they touch the lock. If it doesn’t match the phone quote, you can send them away and call someone else, though you may owe a fair trip fee, so ask on the phone what that is.

After you are back inside

  • Pay by credit card if you can, and keep the itemized invoice (parts, labor, mileage, service charge, business name and address) so you can dispute a charge (BBB).
  • If you think you were scammed, file a complaint with your state attorney general or consumer protection office, the BBB, and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  • Save a trusted locksmith’s number in your phone now. The FTC’s advice is to find a locksmith before you need one (KUTV on the FTC alert).
  • Lost your keys rather than just left them inside? Anyone who finds them could know your address. Ask about rekeying, which usually costs less than replacing the lock ($50 to $130 per lock, per Angi).
